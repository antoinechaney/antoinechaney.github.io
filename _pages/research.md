## **Working Papers**

**Fuel Subsidies and Carbon Emissions in Developing Countries: Evidence from Jordan**

Fossil fuel subsidies remain widespread in developing economies and are often justified on social protection grounds, as they shield households from energy price volatility. However, by artificially lowering energy prices, such subsidies can encourage overconsumption and contribute to rising carbon emissions. Despite their importance, empirical evidence on their environmental consequences remains limited. This paper studies the impact of the 2005 fuel subsidy reform in Jordan on per capita carbon emissions between 1990 and 2010. Using a synthetic control approach, I find that the reform reduced per capita CO₂-equivalent emissions by 0.37 tonnes per year on average over 2006–2010, corresponding to a reduction of 10.54%. This amounts to approximately 2.49 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent abated annually and a cumulative reduction of about 12.5 million tonnes over five years. The reduction in emissions is driven by a decline in energy consumption mainly in the building sector, with notable decreases in diesel and kerosene consumption. I also document a reduction in energy intensity, while carbon intensity remains unchanged, indicating that the reform did not induce a transition toward cleaner fuels.

However, these environmental gains came at significant social costs. The reform sharply increased fuel poverty, particularly among lower-income households, and triggered a shift toward dirtier fuels in rural areas, with reliance on solid fuels rising from 6% in 2002 to 22% in 2008. These results highlight the need to account for distributional impacts when designing subsidy reforms.
